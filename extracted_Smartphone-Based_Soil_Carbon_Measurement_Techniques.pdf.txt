Response: Measuring Soil Organic Carbon (SOC) and Soil Organic Matter (SOM) using smartphone technology represents a transition from
labor-intensive laboratory chemistry (such as dry combustion or Walkley-Black wet oxidation) to rapid, non-destructive, point-of-use sensing.
A smartphone functions as an optical detector, a computational processor, a spatial tagger (GPS/GIS), and an edge/cloud machine-learning
inference engine.
1. What Is Picked Up: Key Signals, Features & Proxies
Smartphones measure soil carbon indirectly by picking up optical, spectral, spatial, and contextual proxies correlated with organic carbon
concentrations.
A. Visible Chromophores and Color Metrics (400–700 nm)
Soil organic matter contains humic and fulvic acids, which are dark-colored organic compounds. As SOC content increases, soil absorbs more
visible light and appears visually darker.
Color Space Transformations: Raw camera RGB values are non-linear and dependent on device hardware. Smartphone algorithms
transform RGB into device-independent color spaces:
CIE  Space:  (Lightness) is the primary proxy. Higher SOC directly correlates with lower  values.
HSV / HSL Space:  (Value) and  (Saturation) quantify soil darkness and color purity.
Munsell Soil Color System: Standardized soil parameters—Hue (dominant spectral color), Value (lightness/darkness from 0 to 10),
and Chroma (color intensity). Soil organic carbon shows a strong inverse relationship with Munsell Value ( ).
Reflectance Ratios: Ratios like , , or normalized indices such as  are extracted from image channels to isolate carbon
absorption from soil matrix background noise.
B. Near-Infrared (NIR) Molecular Overtones (700–2500 nm)
While standard smartphone CMOS sensors capture the visible and very near-infrared spectrum (up to ~1000 nm), specialized smartphone
attachments or paired MEMS sensors read vibrational band overtones:
450–650 nm: Light absorption by chromophores in decomposed organic humic fractions.
1100–1400 nm: C-H first and second overtones associated with aliphatic organic compounds.
1900–2200 nm: Combination bands of O-H, N-H, and C-H stretching in complex soil organic structures (lignin, cellulose, humic
substances).
C. Surface Texture and Structural Proxies
High-resolution macro photography allows computer vision models to evaluate physical soil surface characteristics:
Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM): Measures local textural feature variations (contrast, correlation, energy, homogeneity) that
reflect soil aggregate size and macro-porosity, which co-vary with SOM content.
Micro-shadowing Metrics: Analyzes pixel-level shadow distribution under controlled lighting to differentiate micro-surface roughness from
organic pigment darkening.
D. Contextual and Environmental Covariates
Because soil moisture and mineralogy alter optical signals, modern smartphone frameworks capture additional metadata to calibrate carbon
predictions:
Soil Moisture Content (SM): Water darkens soil in a manner similar to organic matter. Sensor algorithms use moisture compensation
models (or secondary thermal/capacitance sensor feeds) to decouple moisture darkening from carbon presence.
Geospatial & Topographic Metadata: Embedded phone sensors tag samples with GPS location, elevation, and slope. These
coordinates query cloud databases for regional soil series maps, temperature histories, and vegetation indices (e.g., NDVI from satellite
imagery), refining local machine learning models.
2. Historical & Current Implementation Methodologies
Smartphone-based SOC measurement has evolved through four main technological approaches:
Method 1: Pure Digital Image Processing with Color Calibration
Farmers or researchers take a photograph of a prepared soil sample alongside a color calibration card (e.g., X-Rite ColorChecker, Munsell Chip,
or 3D-printed grey scale card) under ambient light or within a portable dark chamber.

Method 2: Smartphone-Mounted Spectrometers (Diffraction Gratings)
Research teams have built low-cost 3D-printed attachments that turn the phone's built-in camera and flash/LED into a visible-light spectrometer.
How It Works: A 3D-printed enclosure snaps onto the phone, holding a slit, collimating lens, transmission diffraction grating, and a sample
cuvette/plate. Light reflected from the soil passes through the grating, splitting into a rainbow spectrum onto the phone's camera sensor.
Software reads spectral line intensities across wavelengths from 400 nm to 700 nm.
Historical Precedent: Systems developed by institutions such as Iowa State University demonstrated that low-cost (<$50) smartphone
spectroscopy modules could achieve spectral resolutions capable of estimating SOM in topsoil.
Method 3: Bluetooth-Connected MEMS NIR Spectrometers
Because standard phone camera sensors drop in sensitivity above 1000 nm, modern commercial setups use compact, battery-powered Micro-
Electro-Mechanical Systems (MEMS) spectrometer hardware paired with smartphones.
How It Works: Devices utilizing silicon-based Fabry-Pérot Interferometers (FPI) or Digital Light Processing (DLP) chipsets emit light in the
900–2500 nm range into the soil. The reflected spectrum is transmitted via Bluetooth to a smartphone application, which sends the
spectral curve to cloud-hosted machine learning models trained on national soil spectral libraries (e.g., USDA-NRCS or ICRAF libraries).
Examples: Devices such as Si-Ware NeoSpectra, Consumer Physics SCiO, and AgroCares Handheld Scanner.
Method 4: Chemical Extract & Leachate Colorimetry ("Cup of Carbon")
For measuring Dissolved Organic Carbon (DOC) in water or soil runoff, smartphones act as transmission colorimeters.
How It Works: Soil is mixed with distilled water, filtered, and placed in a container with a white reference backing. The smartphone
camera analyzes light absorption through the liquid column to calculate DOC concentrations.
3. Data Processing & Machine Learning Pipelines
Converting raw optical readings into accurate SOC percentage estimates ( ) relies on algorithmic processing:
A. Image Preprocessing and Calibration
1. Background Subtraction & Segmentation: Computer vision algorithms isolate uniform soil zones, filtering out roots, stones, and leaf
litter.
2. Illumination Normalization: Polynomial transformations map captured reference card colors to standard illuminant coordinates (e.g., D65
illuminant).
B. Analytical Models
Random Forest Regression (RFR): Frequently outperforms simple linear regressions by capturing non-linear interactions between
 channels, texture GLCM indices, and terrain metadata.
Partial Least Squares Regression (PLSR): Standard for continuous spectral curves generated by clip-on or paired NIR devices,
compressing hundreds of spectral bands into principal components.
Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs): Architectures such as MobileNet or custom lightweight CNNs take raw soil image patches
directly, simultaneously extracting spatial texture and color parameters without manual feature engineering.
C. Moisture Correction Frameworks
Water significantly decreases soil reflectance. To prevent wet soil from causing artificially high SOC predictions, algorithms use:
External Parameter Orthogonalization (EPO): Mathematical projection methods that remove spectral variation directions caused by
changing moisture levels.
Dual-Band Moisture Ratios: Estimating water content from specific absorption bands (e.g., 970 nm or 1450 nm) and adjusting visual
color values back to a dry-equivalent baseline before calculating SOC.

5. Ongoing Challenges and Future Directions
1. Mineral Interference: Soils with high iron oxide content (e.g., red tropical Oxisols/Ultisols) appear dark red or brown, masking organic
carbon chromophores and skewing RGB-based models.
2. Camera Hardware Heterogeneity: Variances in camera CMOS sensors, lens optics, and automatic post-processing pipelines across
smartphone brands require continuous cross-calibration.
3. Depth Limitations: Optical smartphone sensing evaluates surface soil ( ). Accurate carbon stock estimations ( ) require
combining surface readings with depth-extrapolation pedotransfer functions or core-sampling adapters.

B. Shared RGB to CIE  Transformation (commonMain)
package com.terrochroma.vision
import kotlin.math.pow
data class RgbColor(val r: Float, val g: Float, val b: Float) // Range 0.0 - 1.0
data class LabColor(val L: Float, val a: Float, val b: Float)
object ColorSpaceConverter {
    fun rgbToCieLab(rgb: RgbColor): LabColor {
        // 1. Linearize sRGB channels
        fun linearize(c: Float): Float =
            if (c > 0.04045f) ((c + 0.055f) / 1.055f).pow(2.4f) else (c / 12.92f)
        val rL = linearize(rgb.r)
        val gL = linearize(rgb.g)
        val bL = linearize(rgb.b)
        // 2. Convert to XYZ (D65 Standard Observer)
        val x = (rL * 0.4124564f + gL * 0.3575761f + bL * 0.1804375f) / 0.95047f
        val y = (rL * 0.2126729f + gL * 0.7151522f + bL * 0.0721750f) / 1.00000f
        val z = (rL * 0.0193339f + gL * 0.1191920f + bL * 0.9503041f) / 1.08883f
        // 3. Nonlinear transformation for Lab
        fun f(t: Float): Float =
            if (t > 0.008856f) t.pow(1f / 3f) else (7.787f * t) + (16f / 116f)
        val fx = f(x)
        val fy = f(y)
        val fz = f(z)
        val L = (116f * fy) - 16f
        val a = 500f * (fx - fy)
        val b = 200f * (fy - fz)
        return LabColor(L = L, a = a, b = b)
    }
}

2.1 System Purpose & Scope
Radix-C is an edge-native, non-destructive Soil Organic Carbon (SOC) and Soil Organic Matter (SOM) field estimation platform. It runs on Kotlin
Multiplatform (KMP) and Compose Multiplatform (CMP), targeting both Android and iOS devices.
2.2 Key Functional Requirements (FR)
FR-1 (Programmatic Camera Lock): Bypass OS Auto-Exposure and Auto-White-Balance to capture raw, uncompressed Bayer frames
(RAW_SENSOR on Android, AVCapturePhoto Bayer on iOS).
FR-2 (Target Card Calibration): Automatically detect ArUco / AprilTag markers on a physical calibration card, normalising captured pixel
channels to standard D65 illuminants.
FR-3 (Feature Extraction): Convert normalized RGB to CIE , HSV, and Munsell space, extracting GLCM aggregate texture
indicators (contrast, energy, homogeneity).
FR-4 (On-Device ML Inference): Execute lightweight regression models (TFLite / CoreML) offline to output predicted , ,
and calculated carbon stock ( ).
FR-5 (External Spectrometer Support): Interface via Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) with external MEMS NIR scanners (900–2500 nm).

5.1 RGB to CIE  Shared Transformation Logic
package com.radixc.vision
import kotlin.math.pow
data class RgbColor(val r: Float, val g: Float, val b: Float) // Range 0.0 - 1.0
data class LabColor(val L: Float, val a: Float, val b: Float)
object ColorSpaceConverter {
    fun rgbToCieLab(rgb: RgbColor): LabColor {
        // 1. Linearize sRGB channels
        fun linearize(c: Float): Float =
            if (c > 0.04045f) ((c + 0.055f) / 1.055f).pow(2.4f) else (c / 12.92f)
        val rL = linearize(rgb.r)
        val gL = linearize(rgb.g)
        val bL = linearize(rgb.b)
        // 2. Map to XYZ (D65 Standard Observer)
        val x = (rL * 0.4124564f + gL * 0.3575761f + bL * 0.1804375f) / 0.95047f
        val y = (rL * 0.2126729f + gL * 0.7151522f + bL * 0.0721750f) / 1.00000f
        val z = (rL * 0.0193339f + gL * 0.1191920f + bL * 0.9503041f) / 1.08883f
        // 3. Nonlinear transformation for Lab
        fun f(t: Float): Float =
            if (t > 0.008856f) t.pow(1f / 3f) else (7.787f * t) + (16f / 116f)
        val fx = f(x)
        val fy = f(y)
        val fz = f(z)
        val L = (116f * fy) - 16f
        val a = 500f * (fx - fy)
        val b = 200f * (fy - fz)
        return LabColor(L = L, a = a, b = b)
    }
}
